When does a spherical wave stop looking planar?

A satellite illuminates a horizontal ground patch at 30° from zenith. The plane wave is tangent-matched at the patch centre: it already includes the phase slope along the ground. The error below is the curvature it misses.

Distance and patch-size sliders are logarithmic: equal moves multiply their values. Predict first: which curve changes if you choose a shorter wavelength? What if you double the patch width? The two plots have different units; use the numerical errors below them.

Amplitude is normalized to the exact field at the centre. Phase error is unwrapped: we do not hide full cycles by reducing it modulo 2π. The dashed phase guide is π/4 radians (45°), our working tolerance for phase error rather than a sharp physical boundary.

What is being compared?

Source position: (−R sin 30°, R cos 30°). Ground points: (x, 0), with −L ≤ x ≤ L; the vertical coordinate is positive upwards.

Exact source distance: r(x) = √(R² + 2Rx sin 30° + x²).
Local plane-wave distance: rₚ(x) = R + x sin 30°.
Exact amplitude / centre amplitude: R/r(x); plane-wave amplitude / centre amplitude: 1.
Signed amplitude difference: 100[R/r(x) − 1] %.
Phase-error magnitude: (2π/λ)[r(x) − rₚ(x)].

This scalar propagation comparison holds source strength and its angular radiation factor fixed across the patch. It isolates spherical spreading and wavefront curvature, before surface reflection or absorption.
