# Earth as a Magnetic Dipole

A compass samples one field, but that field contains contributions from several parts of the Earth system. Before choosing a model, we should decide which contribution we want it to describe.

## Several sources, one measured field

The dominant, slowly changing contribution is generated by moving, electrically conducting fluid in the **outer core**. Magnetized rocks in the **crust** add spatial structure. Currents in the **ionosphere and magnetosphere** add fields that can change much more rapidly, and changing fields induce currents in the conducting Earth. Moving seawater produces another, weaker contribution. These signals add by superposition; separating them is one of the purposes of ESA's Swarm mission {cite}`esa_swarm_sources`.

Here we begin with the large-scale **core field**, treated as a snapshot. A centred, tilted dipole explains roughly **90% of the present main field in a global mean-square sense**. For a reproducible example, the degree-one dipole part of the International Geomagnetic Reference Field, IGRF-14, accounts for about **93% at epoch 2025.0** on its spherical reference surface. We will compare that dipole with the fuller reference model below {cite}`igrf14_model,igrf14_coefficients`.

This does not mean that the field is reproduced to within 7% at every location. Nor does it mean that the remaining field comes only from the crust or space: the core itself also produces a **non-dipole field**.

```{admonition} Optional: what does “93%” measure?
:class: dropdown

Use an area average over the reference sphere, denoted by angle brackets. Splitting the IGRF field into its dipole part and the remaining spatial patterns gives

$$
\vec B_{\mathrm{IGRF}}=\vec B_{\mathrm{dip}}+\vec B_{\mathrm{rest}}.
$$

These contributions are orthogonal under the global surface average, so

$$
\langle|\vec B_{\mathrm{IGRF}}|^2\rangle
=\langle|\vec B_{\mathrm{dip}}|^2\rangle
+\langle|\vec B_{\mathrm{rest}}|^2\rangle.
$$

For the 2025 coefficients, the dipole fraction of this **mean square** is $0.9286$. The full vector root-mean-square magnitude is about $43.6\,\mu\mathrm T$, and the residual is about $11.7\,\mu\mathrm T$. A fraction of mean-square field is not a fraction of local field amplitude. The calculation is reproduced in {doc}`making_of`.
```

## A dipole is a model of the external field

We replace the complicated core current system by an **equivalent magnetic dipole at Earth's centre**. Its dipole moment specifies a strength and a direction. The model does not require a permanent magnet inside Earth, or a literal single current loop in the core. It describes the leading spatial pattern of the external field.

For the present polarity, field lines enter Earth near the northern end of the dipole axis and leave near its southern end. The magnetic moment $\vec m$ therefore points toward the **southern** end. In the 2025 IGRF dipole, the axis is tilted by about $9.2^\circ$ relative to Earth's rotation axis. The dipole-axis intersections with the surface are not the same as the dip poles of the full field, where a freely suspended needle would be vertical {cite}`bgs_magnetic_poles`.

For the calculation, rotate our coordinates so that the positive $z_m$ axis points along $\vec m$. Define

- $r$: distance from Earth's centre;
- $\theta$: angle measured from the **moment direction**, $0\leq\theta\leq\pi$;
- $\varphi$: azimuth about that axis.

Thus $\theta=0$ is at the southern end of the present Earth dipole, and $\theta=\pi$ is at its northern end. These are **dipole-aligned coordinates**, not geographic latitude and longitude.

## Reuse the dipole potential

We have already connected the dipole field to its potential in Equation {eq}`eq:paired-dipole-potentials`. With the polar axis along $\vec m$, that potential is $\Psi_m=\mu_0m\cos\theta/(4\pi r^2)$.

Let $a$ be our reference Earth radius and define $B_0=\mu_0m/(4\pi a^3)>0$. The **same potential**, expressed using Earth's field-strength scale, is

$$
\boxed{\Psi_m(r,\theta)=B_0\frac{a^3}{r^2}\cos\theta,\qquad r\geq a,}
$$ (eq:earth-dipole-potential)

using the convention

$$
\vec B_{\mathrm{dip}}=-\vec\nabla\Psi_m.
$$

Here $B_0=\mu_0|\vec m|/(4\pi a^3)$, so this is the scalar potential of the point dipole introduced in the previous section. Its units are $\mathrm{T\,m}$, and its zero is chosen so that $\Psi_m\to0$ at infinity. For the 2025 reference dipole, $a=6371.2\,\mathrm{km}$ and $B_0\approx29.7\,\mu\mathrm T$. These are model parameters, not uniform field values everywhere on Earth.

The expression already contains two useful ideas. Its angular dependence is a simple cosine; its radial dependence is $r^{-2}$. Taking a gradient will introduce another inverse power of distance, giving the dipole field its $r^{-3}$ decay.

We use this potential only **outside the source region**, approximating Earth's surface by the sphere $r=a$. Its singularity at the origin lies outside the domain. We are not claiming that the core itself is current-free or described by Laplace's equation.

## Check the equation before accepting the candidate

The exterior internal-source field is both curl-free and divergence-free in our approximation. Its scalar potential must therefore satisfy Laplace's equation. Because our candidate has no azimuthal dependence, the spherical Laplacian reduces to

$$
\nabla^2\Psi_m=
\underbrace{\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}
\left(r^2\frac{\partial\Psi_m}{\partial r}\right)}_{\text{radial contribution}}
+
\underbrace{\frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}
\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial\Psi_m}{\partial\theta}\right)}_{\text{angular contribution}}.
$$ (eq:axisymmetric-magnetic-laplacian)

```{admonition} Check your calculation
:class: dropdown

The radial and angular contributions are, respectively,

$$
\frac{2B_0a^3\cos\theta}{r^4}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
-\frac{2B_0a^3\cos\theta}{r^4}.
$$

They cancel, giving $\nabla^2\Psi_m=0$, even though neither contribution is generally zero separately. The formula is evaluated for $0<\theta<\pi$ and extends smoothly to the axis; the apparent polar singularity belongs to the coordinates, not the field. The origin is a genuine singularity of the point-dipole idealization and is excluded.

The dipole has an orientation and angular variation: a centred source is not necessarily spherically symmetric. The cancellation is another example of **zero Laplacian without constant potential**.
```

Passing this check makes the potential an admissible exterior solution. It does not yet establish that it is the solution for **Earth's boundary data**.

## Recover the field and read it at the surface

For an axisymmetric scalar, the spherical gradient gives

$$
\vec B_{\mathrm{dip}}
=-\frac{\partial\Psi_m}{\partial r}\hat r
-\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\Psi_m}{\partial\theta}\hat\theta.
$$

The first derivatives from the exercise therefore give

$$
\boxed{
B_r=2B_0\left(\frac{a}{r}\right)^3\cos\theta,
\qquad
B_\theta=B_0\left(\frac{a}{r}\right)^3\sin\theta,
\qquad B_\varphi=0.
}
$$ (eq:earth-dipole-components)

At $r=a$, the model field is horizontal at the dipole equator and vertical at the two axis intersections. Its magnitude is

$$
|\vec B_{\mathrm{dip}}(a,\theta)|=B_0\sqrt{1+3\cos^2\theta}.
$$

It is $B_0$ at the equator and $2B_0$ at either pole: approximately $30$ and $60\,\mu\mathrm T$. This explains a major part of the observed variation in direction and strength, using only a moment and an orientation.

```{admonition} Optional: connect to magnetic inclination
:class: dropdown

Define dipole latitude $\lambda_m=\theta-\pi/2$, positive toward the **northern** dipole-axis end. Define inclination $I$ positive downward from the local horizontal. On the reference sphere,

$$
B_{\mathrm{down}}=-B_r=2B_0\sin\lambda_m,
\qquad B_{\mathrm{horizontal}}=B_0\cos\lambda_m.
$$

Hence, away from the poles,

$$
\tan I=2\tan\lambda_m.
$$

The field is horizontal at the dipole equator and dips downward in its northern hemisphere. Geographic latitude cannot simply replace $\lambda_m$ for a tilted dipole; the full geomagnetic field also departs from this simple relation.
```

## A boundary condition we can actually check

Unlike the conducting ground in electrostatics, Earth's surface is **not a magnetic equipotential boundary**. Instead, observations can supply the field's radial component on a reference sphere. Call this boundary function $b_r(\theta,\varphi)$. For the exterior potential, it specifies

$$
-\left.\frac{\partial\Psi_m}{\partial r}\right|_{r=a}
=b_r(\theta,\varphi).
$$ (eq:magnetic-radial-boundary-data)

These are derivative, or **Neumann**, boundary data. The radial direction is normal to the sphere, so prescribing $B_r$ prescribes the potential's normal derivative. Knowing this function **everywhere on the sphere**, together with Laplace's equation in the current-free exterior and $\Psi_m\to0$ at infinity, determines the potential uniquely, provided a solution exists. Its gradient then determines **all three components** of the field. We are not assuming that the field is radial: the components are linked through one scalar potential and cannot be chosen independently.

This also separates two different checks. A magnetic boundary field must have zero **total flux**, but that single integral does not determine the spatial pattern of $B_r$. The Neumann condition specifies that pattern point by point. For testing the dipole model, a mismatch in $B_r$ is already enough to show that it fails to reproduce the full reference field. Comparing tangential components would provide further checks against observations.

```{admonition} Could we use the tangential field instead?
:class: dropdown

Yes, with one additional physical constraint. The tangential field gives the potential's rate of change **along the surface**. If these data are consistent with a scalar potential, integrating along surface paths determines the boundary potential up to a constant. We can then use those potential values as Dirichlet boundary data for the exterior problem.

Here tangential data means the vector tangent to the sphere, with its direction and signed components, not just its magnitude. For the axisymmetric dipole, $B_\varphi=0$, so $B_\theta(\theta)$ supplies the surface variation.

There is a subtle ambiguity. Even when the potential is zero at infinity, adding

$$
\delta\Psi_m=\frac{A}{r}
\qquad\text{adds the field}\qquad
\delta\vec B=\frac{A}{r^2}\hat r.
$$

This potential is harmonic outside the sphere and constant on its surface. It therefore leaves every tangential component unchanged, while changing the radial field. It is not merely a change of potential reference: it changes the field between the boundary and infinity.

The added field has flux $4\pi A$ through the sphere. **No magnetic monopoles** requires zero net flux, so $A=0$. Thus complete, compatible tangential data, the behaviour at infinity and the zero-flux condition also determine the exterior magnetic field. A gravity or electric monopole would be allowed; its strength would need additional information.

We choose $B_r$ because it directly supplies the familiar Neumann condition. Flux is a physical consistency check, not a reason that tangential information is unusable.
```

For our dipole, the predicted radial boundary data are particularly simple:

$$
b_{r,\mathrm{dip}}(\theta,\varphi)=2B_0\cos\theta.
$$

The integral of this pattern over the whole sphere vanishes: flux leaving one hemisphere balances flux entering the other. That is a useful physical check, but it is not enough to match the actual boundary function at every position.

To see what is missing, compare our dipole with **IGRF-14 at epoch 2025.0**, an observation-based model assembled from satellite, observatory and survey information. The comparison below uses its full degree-13 representation of the main field and its degree-one dipole part, both evaluated on $r=a$. The required correction here is about $+2.1\,\mu\mathrm T$ in the outward direction. Other locations require different corrections. The dipole passes the differential-equation check, but does not satisfy the fuller model's boundary data. There is no conflict with uniqueness: uniqueness compares solutions with **the same complete boundary conditions**.

This is useful missing information. The residual tells us to allow more spatial structure in the core-field model, rather than discard Laplace's equation. Actual observations contain further crustal, external and induced contributions, as well as measurement and modelling errors. In the next section we introduce a way to add spatial structure systematically: spherical harmonics.
