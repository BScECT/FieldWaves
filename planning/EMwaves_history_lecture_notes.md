# Historical introduction: lecturer notes, draft 1

Date: 2026-11-01. Branch: `EMwaves`.

Student material: `book/5_electromagnetic_waves/history_maxwell.md`.

## Suggested pacing (60-minute lecture, adjustable)

- 0–7 min: distinguish observations, explanations and predictions; recall source-free potential fields and the unresolved question of propagation time.
- 7–14 min: Ørsted/Ampère; use a compass and wire demonstration or sketch. Ask students to predict the effect of reversing current.
- 14–25 min: Faraday's ring; predict the receiver response at switch-on, steady current and switch-off before revealing observations. Explain flux qualitatively, then give the fixed-loop law.
- 25–34 min: field versus direct-interaction accounts; mechanical-wave analogy and the ether. Ask which parts of a successful model the evidence establishes. Maxwell's rotating model was a heuristic, not an experimentally discovered machine.
- 34–45 min: capacitor consistency check and modern Maxwell equations. Read their physical roles; defer the vector wave-equation derivation. Distinguish this modern teaching route from Maxwell's historical development.
- 45–55 min: Maxwell's independently predicted speed and Hertz's experiments. Relate a reflected standing-wave pattern to the mechanical-wave chapter.
- 55–60 min: end-of-lecture discussion and exit response: give one observation, one historical interpretation, and one testable prediction.

For a 45-minute slot, shorten the model discussion and display the four equations as a reference without manipulating them. Keep the independent-speed comparison and Hertz's spatial evidence.

## Source and presentation choices

Friedel supplies the narrative backbone, not verbatim teaching text. Longair's §§4–5 clarify the provisional mechanical models and the 1861–1862 origin of displacement current. Maxwell's Part I documents serious competing accounts in his own words; Part VI §§95–97 documents the electrical/optical comparison. His paper was read in 1864 and published in 1865. Do not describe a single day in 1865 as the whole discovery.

Show (by following the external link) the Royal Institution's photograph of the actual induction ring and the KIT archive's Hertz apparatus/test record. Images have not been copied into the book, so there is no implied reuse licence. These are optional lecture illustrations, not dependencies for understanding the text.

The historical examples of revised beliefs are (1) extending a static direct-interaction picture into a universal instantaneous account and (2) requiring a mechanical ether. Do not imply that all competing nineteenth-century theories were identical or that Hertz alone disproved every direct-interaction theory or the ether. Maxwell's particular rotating mechanism was explicitly provisional. A good analogy is not automatically a literal belief.

The capacitor argument is labelled as a modern reconstruction. It demonstrates a consistency requirement, not independent experimental proof of every feature of Maxwell's theory. Hertz's receiver spark alone is distinguished from the subsequent spatial evidence for waves; do not present his experiments as a perfectly precise measurement of c.
